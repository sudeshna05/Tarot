The 22 Cards

The Major Arcana

The Major Arcana are the 22 foundational cards of the tarot deck — cards numbered 0 through 21, each representing an archetypal force, a universal human experience, or a significant threshold in the journey of a life.

What Is the Major Arcana?

In a standard 78-card tarot deck, 22 cards belong to the Major Arcana. Unlike the Minor Arcana — which reflects the texture of everyday life — the Major Arcana speaks to larger forces: the deep patterns beneath events, the turning points that shape who we become, and the archetypal experiences common to all human beings.

When Major Arcana cards appear in a reading, they often carry more weight than the cards around them. They suggest that whatever is being asked about carries real significance — something worth sitting with rather than moving past quickly.

The Fool's Journey

The 22 Major Arcana cards can be read as a single narrative: the journey of The Fool (card 0) from innocence through experience to integration (The World, card 21). Along the way, the Fool encounters teachers, challenges, inner forces, and moments of profound transformation. This arc — sometimes called the Fool's Journey — maps something recognizable about how a life unfolds, and how consciousness develops through the full range of human experience.

All 22 Major Arcana Cards

0 · The Fool

The Fool stands at the edge of a cliff, pack over shoulder, ready to step into the unknown. Pure potential and pure naivety in equal measure — a card of beginnings before they have been shaped by experience. It speaks to the courage of starting without knowing the outcome, and the openness that only the truly new possesses.

I · The Magician

The Magician holds all four elemental tools — wand, cup, sword, pentacle — and knows how to use them. A card of will, skill, and the capacity to turn intention into reality. It asks: you have the resources. What are you doing with them?

II · The High Priestess

Veiled and still, the High Priestess sits between two pillars — between what is known and what is concealed. She represents the deep knowing that lives beneath conscious thought: intuition, mystery, and the wisdom that arrives when you stop pushing for an answer. Some things are not meant to be forced into clarity.

III · The Empress

Abundant, sensual, and deeply generative — the Empress embodies the creative force of nature itself. She speaks to fertility in its broadest sense: ideas, projects, relationships, the body, and the natural world. A card of nurturing, growth, and the patience of the seasons.

IV · The Emperor

Where the Empress flows, the Emperor holds. Structure, authority, the establishment of order — the Emperor represents the capacity to build something lasting through discipline and will. At its best, this is steady leadership. At its shadow, it is rigidity that mistakes control for strength.

V · The Hierophant

The keeper of tradition, institution, and received wisdom — the Hierophant stands at the gateway between the individual and the collective. He can represent a mentor, a spiritual tradition, or the values passed down through culture. Reversed, this card often points toward the need to find your own way rather than follow what has simply been given.

VI · The Lovers

More than romance, The Lovers is a card of meaningful choice — the kind of choice that requires you to know what you truly value. Two figures stand at a threshold, a choice laid out before them. The question it asks is not simply "who do you love?" but "what do you choose — and are you willing to live with that choice fully?"

VII · The Chariot

Victory through the force of focused will — two opposing forces harnessed together and driven forward. The Chariot speaks to the moment when you stop being pulled in two directions and commit to a direction, holding competing impulses in productive tension. Movement, determination, and the satisfaction of will made visible.

VIII · Strength

A figure gently holds open the jaws of a lion — not through force, but through a quality of calm, inner certainty. Strength in the tarot is not aggression. It is the quiet power that comes from knowing yourself well enough not to be undone by fear or desire. Patient, compassionate, and deeply grounded.

IX · The Hermit

Alone on a high mountain, lantern raised, the Hermit has withdrawn from the world to seek what only silence can offer. A card of solitude chosen as wisdom — the kind of turning inward that is not loneliness but deep listening. He often appears when external guidance has run out and the only voice left worth following is your own.

X · Wheel of Fortune

The great wheel turns — cycles of fortune and fate in motion, indifferent to individual preference. The Wheel of Fortune reminds us that change is the nature of things: what rises will descend, what has fallen will rise again. Its wisdom is not in resisting the cycle but in understanding where in it you currently stand.

XI · Justice

Balanced scales, an unsheathed sword, an impassive gaze — Justice asks for clear-eyed reckoning. This is a card of cause and effect, of truth without sentiment, of situations being weighed honestly. It neither threatens nor promises — only insists on accountability. What have you done, and what does that mean for what comes next?

XII · The Hanged Man

Suspended upside-down by one foot, the Hanged Man wears an expression not of suffering but of serene revelation. He has chosen to see the world from a completely different angle — and in doing so, has found understanding unavailable to those still rushing through. A card of surrender, pause, and the wisdom that arrives when you stop trying to control the outcome.

XIII · Death

Perhaps the most misunderstood card in the deck — Death rarely speaks of physical death. It speaks of endings: the close of a chapter, the death of an identity, the transformation that is only possible when something is fully let go. What cannot be released cannot be renewed. This card is ultimately about making way for what must come next.

XIV · Temperance

An angelic figure pours water between two cups in perfect balance — neither too much nor too little, neither rushing nor holding back. Temperance is the art of integration: finding the alchemical middle way, working with what is rather than against it. A card of patience, flow, and the long, quiet work of becoming whole.

XV · The Devil

Two figures are chained — but the chains hang loose. They could leave if they wished. The Devil represents the patterns, addictions, and shadow aspects of ourselves that we participate in while insisting we have no choice. This card asks: what are you pretending you cannot free yourself from? The chains are real, but they are also your own.

XVI · The Tower

Lightning strikes a tower, figures fall from its battlements — sudden, unexpected disruption. The Tower appears when something built on an unstable foundation can no longer stand. This is rarely comfortable. But the clearing it creates is real. What the Tower destroys, it destroys because it could not have lasted. What remains is what was always true.

XVII · The Star

After the violence of the Tower comes the quiet of The Star — a naked figure kneeling beside still water, pouring from two vessels, a great star burning above. A card of hope, renewal, and the return of something vital after loss. Not the bright certainty of The Sun, but the quieter, more enduring light of faith restored.

XVIII · The Moon

Night, water, howling dogs, a crayfish climbing from the deep — the Moon governs the realm of the unconscious, where dreams and fears live together. Things are not what they appear to be. Old anxieties surface. The path ahead is uncertain. This card asks you to move through the darkness without insisting on clarity that isn't yet available.

XIX · The Sun

Warmth, clarity, vitality — The Sun brings genuine joy and the satisfaction of things working as they should. Children dance freely, the day is bright, and what has been hidden comes clearly into view. One of the most unambiguously positive cards in the deck, it speaks to a period of openness, success, and the simple pleasure of being alive.

XX · Judgement

Figures rise from open graves in response to an angelic call — a moment of reckoning and awakening. Judgement asks you to look clearly at your life: not with harsh self-criticism, but with the honest willingness to see what worked, what didn't, and what the next chapter is calling you toward. An invitation to answer the call rather than sleep through it.

XXI · The World

A dancing figure at the center of a great wreath, surrounded by the symbols of all four elements — The World is the completion of the journey. Not an ending, but an integration: all the lessons learned, all the forces reconciled, all the pieces of yourself brought into coherent wholeness. One cycle complete; the next already beginning.
